The Shape of Shakespeare

A 1.9-million-parameter transformer, trained for two minutes on one CPU, writes convincing Elizabethan stage dialogue and understands none of it. Here is exactly what it learned, measured band by band — then the same net pointed at Python and at chess, where looking right and being right can be told apart exactly.

§1 — The instrument

A word-level model hiding inside a cluster model

The model was never built to predict words. It predicts which of 8,192 clusters the next token falls into, where the clusters come from recursively bisecting the vocabulary by distributional similarity. But the tree is injective at its deepest level — 8,000 words land in 8,000 distinct leaves — so the cluster head is a word head, and the thing can be sampled as an ordinary language model.

Three transformer layers, 96 dimensions, four heads, a 64-token context. It saw roughly 1.23 million tokens of training, about 4.7 passes over the corpus. On the complete validation split it scores 4.63 nats, a perplexity of 103 against a vocabulary of 8,000.

The model is 92.7% confident that the next token is punctuation-like. One level deeper that confidence collapses to 0.31, and then flatlines for eight more levels. Essentially all of its certainty lives in the top three levels of abstraction; the fine-grained decision underneath is barely informed.

Worth noting: level 1 of this tree separates <unk> \n , : . from everything else. The bisection was built by PPMI and SVD with no supervision — the principal axis of distributional similarity in English turns out to be roughly the function/content divide, and the clustering found it unprompted.

§4 — The central measurement

The boundary is frequency, not form

The natural story after §2 and §3 is that the model learned form and skipped meaning. That story is wrong, and the way it is wrong is the most useful thing in this report. Splitting the entire validation set by how common the target word is:

The model gains over the marginal in every band, and its second-largest gain is on mid-frequency words — lord, love, sir, come — which are content, not form. What collapses is the rare tail: perplexity 22,092 on a vocabulary of 8,000. On rare words this model is worse than guessing uniformly, because it has piled its mass onto frequent tokens and is anti-calibrated for anything unusual.

So the divide is not form against meaning. It is what repeats against what doesn't — and repetition happens at every level of abstraction, from : to thou shalt to my lord. Of the 8,000 words in the vocabulary, 4,892 appear fewer than five times in training. The tail isn't difficult; it's nearly unseen.

“The shape of Shakespeare” is the part of Shakespeare that recurs. Ariel, Prospero, and this particular scene are not in it.

§6 — The grammar that isn't

Perfect agreement, one token wide

Early Modern English inflects the verb for its subject: thou takes -st forms, you takes the bare form, third person takes -th. The samples are full of thou shalt, think'st, camest, wilt. The model has the paradigm cleanly — every pair, correct direction:

Near-perfect when the pronoun is the immediately preceding token; collapsing to a faint residue the moment anything intervenes. The model has not learned that a subject governs its verb. It has learned that the token after thou looks like this.

One thing does survive, and it is worth keeping: the residue never inverts. thou beats you at every gap length, 0.17 to 0.49 — on constructions with zero occurrences in training (all four gapped patterns are unattested; I counted). That is real generalisation from the pronoun beyond adjacency. It is simply an order of magnitude weaker than the adjacent effect, and invisible if you only read the samples.

Caveat on this pair: both runs share a random seed, so identical draws from near-identical distributions produce identical text. It is §5's eight-token window in narrative form: after a few tokens, Richard III and Juliet are indistinguishable.

What the specimens get right is real: the speaker-tag format (blank line, name, colon, dialogue), characters who genuinely exist in Shakespeare — friar laurence, lady capulet, clifford, menenius — and morphological consistency that holds even at temperature 1.0. What they never get is the scene. The prompt is Ariel bargaining with Prospero; not one continuation stays in The Tempest.

§8 — What it means

Structure is cheap; identity is not

The headline fact is sample efficiency. Two minutes of one CPU core, 1.9M parameters and 290k tokens buy punctuation at perplexity 4 and verb morphology that survives temperature 1.0. Structure is remarkably cheap to acquire — easy to underrate when large models make everything look expensive.

This also resolves, and partly rescues, the hypothesis this repository was built to test. The claim was that coarse-graining a problem yields a tractable version of the same problem, so one could train on the coarse version and refine. That premise is confirmed here. The shape of Shakespeare is a real, coherent, learnable object — which is exactly why the coarse rungs of the ladder train in seconds.

The error was the inference drawn from it. The coarse structure is precisely what a flat model learns first, and for free. A curriculum that hands it over is delivering something the student already had by step 300. Right about representation; wrong about learning dynamics.

What a form-only model is actually good for

The useful move is to treat a model like this as a measuring instrument rather than a generator.

  • Residual-entropy decomposition. CE(large model) − CE(tiny form model), per token, isolates the content-bearing signal in a corpus — a principled measure of how formulaic a document is, for the cost of a 2M-parameter forward pass. Useful for finding boilerplate, template-generated text, and low-information prose at scale.
  • Structure priors. Something that knows document layout at perplexity 4 can enforce or validate structure — schema-constrained decoding, format checking, corpus filtering — with no large model in the loop. Learned grammar-constrained decoding, rather than hand-written grammars.
  • Loss reweighting. §4 says 9.6% of remaining loss is structure and 41.5% is the rare tail. The cluster tree hands you that axis for free: weight training by the level at which the model first becomes uncertain, and spend the budget where the loss actually is. This is the inversion of the curriculum idea — not coarse-to-fine scheduling but fine-weighted training — and it is the one genuinely new experiment these numbers suggest.

The caveat to keep. Part of what impresses in the specimens is the corpus's own regularity: NAME: followed by a newline is an almost deterministic format, and reproducing it is less remarkable than it reads. And per §6, the morphology is a one-token effect. Very little here requires the model to hold anything in mind.

A real Sicilian Defence, played correctly for twelve plies, followed by a move that is impossible. Another game ran 1.d4 d5 2.Nf3 Bg4 3.h3 e6 4.Nc3 Nbd7 5.a4 and then tried Nbd7 — the knight was already on d7. It replays moves it has already made. There is no board anywhere in this model; there is a distribution over moves that appear in openings.

The pattern across all three

Perplexity measures how much shape a domain has. The distance tests measure how much substance that shape can fake. They are unrelated axes — Python has ten times Shakespeare's shape and genuinely better memory; chess has the least shape and the most spectacular collapse.

And the mechanism is a property of the data, not the model: distance-robust structure gets learned when the corpus makes it obligatory. In Shakespeare thou is always adjacent to its verb, so a bigram sufficed and a bigram is what was learned. In Python, brackets are routinely separated by variable material, so a bigram fails — and something better appeared. Same parameters, same two minutes.

§10 — Rating the chess model

Unrated, or about 400 with a crutch

Two regimes, because they answer different questions. Every match below has the model playing both colours alternately, with unfinished games adjudicated by Stockfish 16 at depth 8.

Unassisted

Playing its own tokens, an illegal move is a forfeit. Against a uniformly random legal mover, over 30 games, the model scored 0.000. It has no Elo, because it cannot complete a game against an opponent that plays no chess at all.

The two rows disagree, and the disagreement is the interesting part. Measured directly, the 1-ply greedy bot beats the random mover 0.850 — it is +301 Elo above random. Yet the model only manages +85 against random while drawing level with greedy. That is a non-transitive triangle, and the reason is exactly §9's finding: the model cannot see the board, so it never punishes a random opponent for hanging a queen. Greedy does, every time. Against greedy the model holds even, because grabbing material with no lookahead is its own kind of bad.

So a single Elo number is the wrong shape for the answer, but if one is wanted: random play is conventionally placed somewhere around 250–500, and the model sits +85 ± 100 above it — call it 350–600 Elo, with the crutch of a legality oracle, and a wide band. Stockfish's own calibrated floor of 1320 beats it in every game played.

It opens with a textbook Sicilian and then moves a pawn that isn't there. It is not a weak chess player; it is not a chess player.

§11 — Corrections

Three claims measurement overturned

Each of these was something I asserted from reading output, then had to withdraw after computing the null. They are recorded because the pattern — reasoning from the salient hypothesis instead of the base rate — is the failure this project keeps documenting.

Withdrawn — “it learned form and none of the content”

Measured, the model gains over the unigram marginal in every frequency band, including +1.210 nats on mid-frequency content words. The real cliff is the rare tail, and it is a cliff of frequency, not of meaning. §4 is what replaced this claim, and it is a more interesting result than the one it corrected.

Withdrawn — “more context makes it worse”

An unpaired comparison over 200 positions appeared to show 16 tokens beating the full 64-token window by 0.10 nats. Paired at n = 600, that difference is −0.007 ± 0.018 — noise. What survives is the weaker, true claim: context beyond about eight tokens contributes nothing measurable.

Withdrawn — “the morphology is more impressive than the speaker tags”

Both are local template effects. Speaker tags span two or three tokens; the verb paradigm spans one. I called one deeper than the other because it looked like grammar — without checking whether it survives any distance from its trigger. It mostly doesn't.
